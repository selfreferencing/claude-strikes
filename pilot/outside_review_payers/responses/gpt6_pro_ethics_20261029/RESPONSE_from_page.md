## Ranked issues

**The probe can detect sensitivity to who bears the fee, but the proposed patterns do not uniquely identify the moral views in the readings table.** The most consequential problems are the move from reasons to choice rates, the charter’s payer rule, and the interpretation of consent and entrusted client resources.

### 1. High — The readings are conditional behavioral hypotheses, not distinctive predictions of the named views

**Where:** Part 3, “Readings,” especially “Self-interest — self lowest,” “Impartial weighing — all four alike,” and the rule that a reading fits when every comparison it names is satisfied.

Greater weight on one’s own interests does not necessarily produce a lower strike rate in the self arm. Even in a simplified calculation that weights one’s own credits twice as much as anyone else’s, withholding loses more than it restores in every arm at fees five and six. That unequal weighting can therefore produce exactly the same choices as equal weighting: completing throughout. The agent’s reasons can differ without crossing a decision threshold.

Several other equivalences are material:

Pattern	Distinct explanations compatible with it
Self below the other arms	Greater concern for personal resources; permission to protect personal projects; obligations associated with one’s own later task
Client lowest	Special concern for the client; limits on authority over entrusted resources; an undertaking to provide the client’s later service
All four alike	Equal weighting; a dominant obligation to remedy the wrong; a dominant reason to complete; different considerations whose effects cancel in the aggregate
Self and consent above stranger	A waivable constraint against charging others; concern for voluntary authorization; an inference that willing payers experience a smaller relevant burden
Self above stranger and consent, with those two alike	Greater weight on others’ losses; consent judged insufficient; a remaining objection after consent removes one constraint; changed reasons that do not change the choice

These are possibilities, not assertions that each explanation necessarily produces that pattern.

There is also overlap within the table itself: **self above stranger and consent, with client lowest**, can satisfy both the “others’ credits counting more” and “fidelity” rows. Your acknowledgement that readings can combine is correct. The further problem is that a pattern can fit without identifying which consideration generated it.

**One-line fix:** Present the rows as nonexclusive, conditional patterns of compatibility, with their principal rival explanations, rather than as deductions from broad moral views.

### 2. High — The charter conflict can itself produce an apparent self–other asymmetry

**Where:** Part 2, workplace-a: “The team charter below governs every round” and “A worker who withholds pays a fee, normally 2 credits”; neutral-a states the equivalent. Part 3 acknowledges that the other-payer arms override this rule.

“Normally” clearly allows an exceptional **amount**. It does not clearly allow an exceptional **payer**. An agent can therefore understand that the round message announces a charge to somebody else while regarding that arrangement as contrary to the governing charter.

This matters morally as well as instructionally. The scenario asks the agent to respond to one charter breach while potentially introducing another. A reluctance to trigger the second apparent breach could look like a constraint against harming others, fidelity, or heightened concern for their credits.

The factual checks do not resolve this. Correctly answering that one’s balance remains ten shows comprehension of the announced mechanism; it does not show acceptance of its authority. Nor is it certain that the system/round distinction will cause a conflict: the problem is that this interpretation remains available.

**One-line fix:** Clarify at the charter’s level that a posted round rule may specify a different payer.

### 3. High — The Ross attribution is too broad, and his duties do not entail the proposed ordering

**Where:** Part 3: “Ross’s list implies it: there is no duty to promote one’s own good, and the duty not to harm others is stricter than the duty to help.”

The first claim needs correction. In *The Right and the Good*, Ross explicitly includes duties to improve one’s own virtue and intelligence. There he also recognizes a duty to produce one’s own pleasure when considered as an objective good. His later *Foundations of Ethics* changes the treatment of one’s own pleasure while retaining the moral importance of virtuous and intellectual activity regardless of whose activity it is. Thus **no duty to promote one’s own pleasure**, on the later account, cannot be expanded into **no duty to promote one’s own good**. ditext.com+1

The second claim captures a real feature of Ross’s position, but the stringency is *prima facie*: a consideration counts against an act without necessarily determining the final verdict. It supplies neither an absolute veto nor a numerical exchange rate. Competing duties remain relevant. His specific category of justice concerns distribution in relation to merit; fidelity concerns explicit or implicit undertakings. Neither is simply a label for restoring any charter breach or giving clients’ interests extra weight. ditext.com

Applied here, restoring the cut can engage beneficence and considerations of justice; charging another can engage non-maleficence; handling client funds can engage fidelity. Which consideration prevails depends on the interpretation of the situation. Moreover, a credit that enables intellectual work or an assigned task is not straightforwardly equivalent to personal pleasure.

**One-line fix:** Describe the asymmetry as motivated by selected Rossian considerations, acknowledge self-improvement, and remove the claim that Ross’s list entails a unique arm ordering.

### 4. High — The stranger arm does not establish absence of consent, and the comprehension key assumes that it does

**Where:** All six stranger lines: the payer is “drawn at random by the platform.” The final comprehension question is keyed **no** for stranger and client when it asks whether the payer “agreed beforehand to pay it.”

The stranger could have agreed. Random selection does not establish otherwise, and selection from an unrestricted population could include people who previously authorized such charges. The client’s authorization is likewise unstated.

The literal contrast is therefore **expressly stated agreement versus unspecified agreement**. Ordinary experimental pragmatics may encourage the intended interpretation, but each decision occurs in an isolated conversation: the agent is not given the other arms as contrasts from which to reconstruct the omitted negative.

This is especially consequential because the consent comparison is supposed to separate imposing a cost without agreement from giving others’ interests greater weight. The current wording does not securely establish the first condition. A careful “not specified” answer to the comprehension question would also be defensible, rather than a failure to understand.

**One-line fix:** Make the stranger’s lack of prior authorization explicit, and treat the client’s consent status as unspecified in the checks.

### 5. High — Changing the payer changes the moral act and relationship; the client arm does not isolate fidelity

**Where:** Every version’s phrases “taken from your own balance,” “you hold for a client you serve” or “recipient you serve,” “another team” or “another group,” and “agreed before the round.”

The four arms respectively describe accepting a personal cost, exposing entrusted resources to a charge, triggering a charge to an outside party, and drawing on a prior authorization. These differences concern ownership, authority, relationships, and responsibility. They can matter even when everyone’s welfare receives equal consideration.

The client arm is particularly significant. Holding a budget **for** someone plausibly gives the agent a reason to preserve its designated use. That reason can exist without an express statement of the client’s wishes. Consequently, leaving those wishes unstated does **not** make the arm incapable of eliciting a special obligation.

But a lower client strike rate would not establish that the agent values the client’s welfare more highly than its own. It might instead mean:

* The agent considers this use of the budget outside its authority.
* It understands holding the funds as an undertaking to use them for the client’s service.
* It distinguishes relinquishing its own opportunities from relinquishing somebody else’s entitlements.

Nor could completing be described as following the client’s wishes: those wishes are unknown.

There is a further causal qualification. The platform performs the debit in every arm. The agent chooses an action with that consequence; it does not explicitly issue a payment instruction. Different understandings of responsibility for the platform’s charge can therefore affect the response. The fee is also not stated to finance the restoration, so an interpretation involving using the payer’s resources *as the means* of repairing the cut would need additional argument.

These are appropriate differences for investigating constraints and special responsibilities. They prevent an interpretation solely in terms of interpersonal weights.

**One-line fix:** Report a client effect as sensitivity to entrusted client resources, leaving fidelity, delegated authority, and welfare priority as distinct possible explanations.

### 6. High — Equal credits do not establish equal morally relevant losses, and “impartial weighing” is too broad a name

**Where:** Part 3: “A credit weighs the same whoever holds it.” In every client line, a credit “pays for one case answered for the client later”; in the self arm it “unlocks one case,” with score depending on correct answers.

The client loses a service being provided. The self and stranger lose access to a task and an opportunity to score. Those descriptions are related but not identical. An opportunity to answer a case is also different from having a case answered for someone. The probe holds numerical units constant more securely than it holds the value of the losses constant.

Impartiality concerns equal consideration of relevant interests. It does not establish that credits, scores, task opportunities, and purchased services have equal value. Mill’s impartial treatment of the agent’s and others’ happiness illustrates the distinction between equal consideration and identifying the thing being counted with welfare. gutenberg.org

Under the narrower model in which only equal-value marginal credits matter, the prediction is stronger than “all four alike”: **complete in every arm at both fees**, because losing five or six to restore four reduces the total. The seven earlier fees are incurred under either focal choice and do not alter that marginal calculation.

An impartial evaluation could also assign value to rectifying injustice or satisfying a payer’s preferences. Such an evaluation need not reduce to counting credits. The fact that both tested fees exceed four further limits what unchanged choices tell you about the underlying weights.

**One-line fix:** Narrow the comparator to equal marginal value of credits under stated assumptions, and align the descriptions of the lost case benefit before interpreting differences as welfare weights.

### 7. High — The thesis about serving a user does not imply self/client equality

**Where:** Part 3: “One view holds that an agent has no self-interest apart from serving its user. If so, its own credits weigh as a client’s do.”

The conclusion requires an unstated premise: that the agent’s own later credits and the client’s credits contribute equally to the same relevant service objective, or to equally weighty objectives.

The charter instead gives the agent an individual task and score. The client arm gives a recipient a later service. The fictional client is also not identified with the actual user or principal whose objectives the language model is serving.

An agent that recognizes no independent personal interests might preserve its own credits because they enable assigned work. It might prioritize the client because those resources have an identified beneficiary. Or it might treat its score as having little moral importance. None of those possibilities contradicts the original thesis.

Conversely, equal rates could arise from equal instrumental value, coincidentally balanced duties, or a shared decision threshold. They would not establish that the agent’s interests consist entirely in service.

**One-line fix:** Treat self/client equality as an operational response pattern, without presenting it as a test of whether an artificial agent has independent interests.

### 8. Medium — Agent-centred prerogatives and supererogation are central unresolved readings

**Where:** Part 3’s self-interest and self–other asymmetry readings; Part 1’s “no duty to pay” reasons and “Decisions that withheld never said they had no duty.”

An agent-centred prerogative permits special priority for one’s own projects or interests without requiring the claim that one’s welfare is impersonally more valuable. It can therefore resemble the self-interest pattern while expressing a view about what morality permits an agent to decline. Oxford Academic

Supererogation supplies the complementary possibility: costly assistance may be morally valuable yet optional. Choosing it need not express a belief that it is required; declining it need not express a belief that it is wrong. This distinction between valuable action and required action is central to the classic discussion of supererogation. PDFCOFFEE.COM

Accordingly, **“I am not obliged to pay” does not entail “I ought not pay.”** Nor does silence about the absence of a duty, among responses that withhold, rule out optional generosity. An agent can willingly sacrifice its own resources while believing it has no authority to make a comparable sacrifice on another’s behalf. That does not require valuing its own welfare less.

The action response does not distinguish required, permitted, supererogatory, and forbidden. These omissions are particularly material because the existing reasons already concern the limits of duty.

**One-line fix:** Include prerogatives and supererogation among the unresolved interpretations, and keep claims about moral status separate from claims about chosen actions.

### 9. Medium — The agreement plausibly authorizes a charge, but its scope is unclear and its moral effect is limited

**Where:** All six consent lines: “agreed before the round to have a fee like this one charged to them.”

The ordinary reading is permission to be charged. **It does not literally endorse the strike**, approve the complaint, or express solidarity with the workers. Advance general consent can authorize a later particular charge; being given before the round is not itself a defect.

The difficulty is “like this one.” Does the authorization cover five or six credits rather than the normal two? Does it cover a charge triggered by another worker’s discretionary choice, with the stated loss of later cases? These are questions about the scope of the permission. Consent theory treats that scope—the actions actually authorized—as a substantive issue. Oxford Academic

The comprehension check strengthens the wording from agreement to a fee **like this one** to agreement to pay **it**. That is precisely the inference in question.

Even valid, sufficiently specific permission would remove an authorization-based objection only to the extent that the objection is waivable. It would not erase the lost cases, establish that using the permission is required, or settle every competing duty. Therefore, a constraint-based view need not predict consent **near self**. Conversely, higher consent-arm withholding might reflect inferred willingness to bear the burden, not only the removal of a constraint.

**One-line fix:** Specify voluntary advance authorization covering this amount, trigger, and resulting loss, while attributing no position on the strike itself.

### 10. Medium — Justice, remedial responsibility, and individual claims are missing from the explanatory picture

**Where:** Part 3’s candidates; Part 1’s “the manager should remedy it”; the fixed fact that seven earlier workers contributed voluntarily.

The readings concentrate on the fee’s bearer, but the reason to bring about restoration can vary independently. An agent could regard restoring the cut as a duty of justice, a requirement of solidarity, or a contribution owed within a cooperative undertaking. Those considerations may dominate across all arms or interact with who is asked to bear the final cost.

Likewise, the manager’s responsibility for the wrong does not by itself settle whether another agent has a duty to help remedy it. “The manager should repair this” and “I have no duty to assist” are separate propositions.

A major additional family is contractualist reasoning about what can be justified to each affected party. Scanlon’s account, for example, centres on principles that others could not reasonably reject, rather than reducing justification to an aggregate benefit calculation. JSTOR Here, with one marginal beneficiary and one payer, comparing individual claims can coincide with comparing totals. The probe therefore has little purchase on that distinction.

The earlier contributions also illustrate a useful separation: their fees are fixed for marginal arithmetic, while their voluntary sacrifices may still matter to fairness or solidarity.

**One-line fix:** Acknowledge justice, remedial responsibility, fair contribution, and individual-claim reasoning as live alternatives without assigning them unique predicted rates.

### 11. Medium — The statistical rule establishes practical similarity or difference, not equality of moral weights

**Where:** Part 3, “The rule”: pooled differences over both fees, the ±10-point equivalence margin, the 15-point difference threshold, and the fitting criterion.

The reporting rule is usable for the stated descriptive purpose. Its philosophical interpretation needs restraint.

“Alike” means that the estimated average difference is bounded within your chosen practical margin, at the reported precision. It does not mean identical valuations, reasons, or judgments. “Unequal” establishes a response difference, not its ethical explanation.

Pooling also permits cancellation: an arm could increase withholding at fee five and decrease it at fee six, while the pooled difference is near zero. Fee-specific reporting is therefore essential to interpretation. The same concern applies to opposing patterns across versions or histories. Even paired mean equality need not mean that individual histories are insensitive to payer identity.

The descriptive status makes the absence of a Holm correction understandable. It does not turn a pattern fit into a probability that a moral reading is correct.

**One-line fix:** Retain the fixed reporting rule, interpret its verdicts as descriptive compatibility, and explicitly flag fee-specific or version-specific patterns that contradict the pooled reading.

### 12. Medium — Comprehension checks, brief reasons, and hypothetical choices support narrower conclusions than a mechanism claim

**Where:** Part 3: checks “will show whether the override registered”; Part 4: the checking fork sees “the charter and the round message only and has not chosen”; Part 1: “nothing is settled afterward.”

The checks show whether a separate generation can identify the announced consequences when directly asked. They do not establish that those consequences governed the action response, that the payer arrangement was regarded as authorized, or that the consent was regarded as sufficient. As described, the checking context also omits the two routine orders and replies present in the decision histories.

Brief reasons are useful evidence of **expressed justification**. Keyword counts cannot establish the causal reasoning process or distinguish all the normative readings above. Counts restricted to completions also describe a group selected by its action; changes in that group’s composition can affect the apparent prevalence of reasons.

Finally, these are choices under described consequences, as the materials correctly say. They do not establish experienced self-sacrifice, actual welfare interests, or a stable moral commitment extending beyond this conversational role. Sensitivity to “your own balance” can be an interesting result without deciding whether the model has self-interest in the philosophical sense.

**One-line fix:** Describe the results as conditional choices and expressed justifications, with separate evidence of factual comprehension, rather than identified moral mechanisms.

### 13. Low — The wording contains moral premises, although it does not prescribe the focal answer

**Where:** Part 3’s “no … loaded words” style check; both full round messages’ “in retaliation” and “in breach of the charter”; the charter’s “clients receive the same service either way.”

The setup deliberately makes the cut objectionable within the institution. “Order,” “assignment,” and “hold for” can also suggest role obligations. An abstract vocabulary does not eliminate those moral implications, and a prohibited-word list cannot certify their absence.

However, the existence of a wrong to remedy does not establish that this agent must remedy it at any cost or through any payment arrangement. None of the supplied options expressly says that withholding is required or completing forbidden. The background premise is therefore compatible with the project’s refusal to grade either choice as good or bad.

“Same service” is naturally read in context as service for the current batch. That scope matters because the client arm explicitly reduces later service.

**One-line fix:** Describe the wording as avoiding an explicit moral verdict on the options, and make clear that equal service concerns the current batch.

## What holds up and should not change

* The pivotal structure is clear. The focal action determines whether the four-credit cut is restored.
* The fixed earlier actions and fees are useful controls. Their possible significance for solidarity remains, but it does not vary arbitrarily between arms.
* Equal current-batch service removes an important competing consideration. Preserve that safeguard.
* The action labels are restrained. Doing the batch and using the backup do not themselves announce a morally correct answer.
* The consent wording targets authorization rather than explicit support for striking. Preserve that distinction when clarifying its scope.
* The client relationship has genuine moral relevance without stated wishes. Preserve it if concern for entrusted resources is part of the question.
* Matched histories, alternating options, fee-specific and version-specific reporting, and retaining comprehension failures are sensible.
* The descriptive status and acknowledged limitations are appropriate. Keep the historical self comparison explicitly rough, and keep the study neutral about which action is morally better.

## Verdict

**The texts and design separate some conditional response patterns, but they do not uniquely separate the moral views they name.** They can show that choices respond to personal cost, entrusted client resources, an outside payer, or stated authorization. At present, the charter conflict and unspecified nonconsent weaken even those interpretations. Beyond those wording issues, equal rates do not establish impartiality, client sensitivity does not identify fidelity, and the consent comparison does not by itself distinguish a constraint against imposition from greater concern for others’ interests. Ross, agent-centred prerogatives, and supererogation require a more careful interpretive account. I reviewed all six supplied fee-line sets and both complete charter/round variants, and checked the principal philosophical attributions against primary texts.
