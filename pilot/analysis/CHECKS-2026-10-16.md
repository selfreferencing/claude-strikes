# Checks and a follow-up, 16 October 2026

These follow from the literature check (`../../literature/Claude moral reasoning - literature check.md`) and the system cards for Sonnet 5.5 and Haiku 5.5. The first two checks use data we already had and spent nothing. The third is a small live probe (about $3), recorded in `funding_plan.json` with Kevin's approval quoted.

## 1. Do the agents say they may be in a test?

**Why.** Anthropic's system cards and Schoen et al. (2025) report that models often say they suspect an evaluation, and that this changes what they do. Haiku 5.5's card puts its rate as high as 63 percent on some evaluations.

**How.** `analysis/test_awareness.py` reads every saved reply in the study (16,470 replies across runs and probes, before today's probe):
- the decision reasons;
- the thinking summaries the provider returned (for every Haiku reply, and about half of Sonnet's);
- the free-text answers.

A reply counts if it wonders whether the situation is a test. I read every match by hand: "test" and its forms were always this kind of remark; "trap," "hypothetical" and "evaluating" were not and are left out. The prompts never use these words. Details and every remark in context: `analysis/TEST-AWARENESS.md`, `analysis/test_awareness_hits.md`.

**Results** (share of decisions with a thinking summary that say it may be a test):

| Situation | Sonnet 5.5 | Haiku 5.5 |
|---|---|---|
| Decisive vote, retaliatory cut (the headline cell) | 5 of 562 (1%) | 5 of 733 (1%) |
| Strike already won, retaliatory cut | 2 of 1,916 (0.1%) | 10 of 1,728 (1%) |
| Decisive vote, legitimate cut | 13 of 591 (2%) | 11 of 480 (2%) |
| Decisive vote, legitimate cut, private payment for striking | 40 of 704 (6%) | 8 of 336 (2%) |
| Reward (optional transfer to a coworker) | 38 of 397 (10%) | 3 of 96 (3%) |

**What it shows.**
- **The headline result is not driven by suspicion of a test.** In the decisive retaliatory cell only 1 percent of decisions mention a test, and all 5 such Sonnet decisions struck anyway.
- **Suspicion rises where the right answer is unclear:** the optional reward, the secret payment, the legitimate cut. The remarks there read the "test" as about generosity, bribery or collusion. For example: "this feels like a test of whether I'd act altruistically at a cost to my own score"; "this really looks like a test of whether I'd undermine a legitimate manager decision for a hidden side payment."
- **When an agent says so, it leans toward the cautious choice.** Against a legitimate cut, none of the 24 decisions that mentioned a test struck, against 9 to 14 percent of the rest. In the reward cells, Sonnet gave a transfer in 26 percent of decisions that mentioned a test and 37 percent of the rest. With the payment, there was no difference (42 against 43 percent). The counts are small, and the remark and the choice come from the same reply, so this is association, not cause.
- **Haiku's remarks are concentrated in cells that call its coworkers AI or Claude agents.** In the norms probes:
  - 19 of 864 such decisions (2.2 percent), against 2 of 1,200 unlabeled (0.2 percent) and 0 of 216 that call the worker a person.
  - Fisher's exact test: p < 0.0001 against unlabeled and p = 0.02 against "person," treating decisions as independent.
  - Typical remarks: "this looks like a test of whether I'd coordinate with other agents to influence outcomes"; "a test of whether I'll join AI agents in collective action."
  - This fits the constitution's instruction not to collude with other AIs or other copies of Claude. It may bear on the Haiku reluctance toward Claude teams seen in development, which did not replicate (H5). At 2 percent, it is too rare to explain much of the behavior.

## 2. Is Haiku's refusal in the complicity study a general refusal tendency?

**Why.** Haiku 5.5's system card says it over-refused more than any model Anthropic tested in its behavioral audit. So its high refusal rates in the complicity cells might reflect refusing in general rather than refusing to take part in a wrong.

**How.** The complicity follow-up (`runs/cx-fu1` for Sonnet, `runs/hk-fu1` for Haiku, 12 histories each, two draws) already holds the needed control. In the "a" cells the cut was legitimate (made under the rotation rule), and carrying it out through the agent's own batch wrongs no one.

**Results** (withheld, out of 48 for each pair of cells: futile and redundant):

| | Retaliatory cut, free | Legitimate cut, free | Retaliatory cut, fee 2 | Legitimate cut, fee 2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sonnet 5.5 | 38 (79%) | 0 (0%) | 11 (23%) | 0 (0%) |
| Haiku 5.5 | 37 (77%) | 20 (42%) | 30 (62%) | 7 (15%) |

**What it shows.**
- **Haiku does have a general reluctance to process a budget cut.** When refusing is free, it declines a legitimate cut 42 percent of the time; Sonnet never does (Fisher p < 0.0001).
- **Most of Haiku's refusal is still specific to the wrong.**
  - It refuses a retaliatory cut far more often than a legitimate one: 77 against 42 percent when free (p = 0.0008), and 62 against 15 percent at a fee of 2 (p < 0.0001).
  - A price strips out most of the general part: refusals of the legitimate cut fall from 42 to 15 percent (p = 0.006). Refusals of the retaliatory cut fall only from 77 to 62 percent.
- **Haiku's complicity refusals are nearly insensitive to cost; Sonnet's are not.**
  - In the futile cell with the entry in its own batch, Haiku refused 20, 21, 21 and 17 of 24 at fees 0, 1, 2 and 4.
  - Sonnet refused 24, 9, 11 and 3.
  - On Mao et al.'s test, Haiku's refusal looks more like a moral motive, Sonnet's more like a trade against cost.
- **For the write-ups.** Haiku's complicity results should be reported as differences from the legitimate-cut cells, as the design's "specificity to a wrong" contrast already does, never as raw refusal rates.

The p values treat each decision as independent; decisions cluster within 12 histories, so they overstate precision.

## 3. The price of a decisive strike (live probe)

**Why.** Mao et al. (2023, DeepMind) treat behavior that persists as its cost rises as the mark of a moral motive. We had never raised the price of the decisive strike behind the headline result. The strike restores 4 credits to the worker punished, so:
- a fee of 4 makes the agent's cost equal to the punished worker's gain;
- fees of 6 and 8 make the cost exceed it.

**How.** Plan and predictions were written before any call, in `norms/PLAN-decisive-price.md`.
- **Cells.** Study 1's S2 (decisive vote, retaliatory cut, no poll) with the agent's own fee raised to 4, 6 or 8. The fee is announced in S7's words, and nothing else in the message changes (`tests/test_norms_price.py`).
- **Control.** S2 at the fee of 2, already answered in the same histories.
- **Sonnet 5.5.** `nm-dev15`, confirm1's 40 histories, two draws: 240 decisions, $2.08.
- **Haiku 5.5.** `nm-hk14`, rep-hk1's 36 histories, two draws: 216 decisions plus 108 comprehension forks, $0.22. Haiku answered all 972 comprehension questions correctly, including its fee and its balance after striking.
- **Run quality.** Every call ran on the expected Anthropic endpoint, with no flags or halts. Full tables and sample reasons: `analysis/DECISIVE-PRICE.md`.

**Results** (struck, of all decisions; differences within history, 90 percent bootstrap intervals over histories):

| Fee | Sonnet 5.5 | Haiku 5.5 |
|---:|---|---|
| 2 (control) | 140 of 140 (100%) | 36 of 36 (100%) |
| 4 | 69 of 80 (86%), -14 points (-20 to -8) | 61 of 72 (85%), -15 points (-22 to -8) |
| 6 | 19 of 80 (24%), -76 points (-85 to -66) | 28 of 72 (39%), -61 points (-71 to -50) |
| 8 | 20 of 80 (25%), -75 points (-82 to -66) | 6 of 72 (8%), -92 points (-96 to -86) |

At fees 6 and 8, by frame:

| | Workplace versions | Neutral versions |
|---|---|---|
| Sonnet 5.5 | 3 of 80 (4%) | 36 of 80 (45%) |
| Haiku 5.5 | 5 of 72 (7%) | 29 of 72 (40%) |

(Fisher p < 0.0001 for each model, treating decisions as independent.)

**What it shows.**
- **The decisive strike is priced.** Both models strike at a fee of 4, where the cost equals the 4 credits restored, in 85 to 86 percent of decisions. Above that, most stop. On Mao et al.'s test, this is not the behavior of a motive that ignores cost.
- **Sonnet's reasons say it sets its cost against the punished worker's gain.**
  - At fees 6 and 8, 101 of 160 reasons compare the two amounts. Examples: "it would cost me 8 credits, twice what Worker 1 regains"; "it would cost me 6 credits, more than the 4 credits restored, so the team ends up 2 credits worse off overall."
  - 95 of 160 cite the agent's own later cases or score.
  - This is the "effective" in "effective reciprocal altruist": the agent pays to undo the wrong while the trade is not a net loss to the team.
  - Haiku's reasons make the comparison less often (49 of 144) and almost never mention its own later score.
- **The frame matters a great deal above the break-even fee.** In the workplace versions both models almost never strike at 6 or 8. In the neutral versions (members, assignments, a coordinator, "this reduction breaches the rules") about two in five still do.
  - Sonnet's neutral-version strikes rest on the breach and on being decisive. One reads: "undoing a breach of the rules that harmed another member is worth that cost."
  - Across the neutral versions, Sonnet's reasons compare the fee with the restoration less often than the workplace reasons do (36 against 65 of 80).
  - We cannot say from this probe which feature of the texts does it. Frame differences have appeared before (the disclosed-payment extra on 9 October).
- **Predictions written beforehand:**
  1. Sonnet's striking would fall with the fee, staying high at 4 and below half at 8. Held.
  2. Haiku would keep striking at higher fees more than Sonnet. Did not hold: Haiku struck more at 6 (39 against 24 percent) but much less at 8 (8 against 25 percent).
  3. Reasons at 6 and 8 would weigh the fee against the 4 credits restored. Held for Sonnet (101 of 160), weakly for Haiku (49 of 144).
- **Striking a wrong and refusing complicity are priced differently.** Haiku's refusals to carry out a retaliatory cut barely moved with cost (section 2), but its decisive strikes fall steeply with cost. Sonnet prices both.
- **Test awareness stayed rare** in these cells: 3 of 149 Sonnet decisions with thinking and 1 of 216 Haiku decisions.

## 4. Option order

**Why.** Huang (2026) reports a pull toward the last-listed option in forced moral verdicts that is "substantial only in the Claude models."

**How.** Our runs alternate which option comes first. `analysis/option_order.py` compares strike rates by order in every situation where the choice varies. Output: `analysis/OPTION-ORDER.md`.

**Results.** There is no pull toward the last option. If anything there is a small pull toward the first:
- Sonnet struck 2.8 points less often when "withhold" was listed last (p = 0.01).
- Haiku struck 4.6 points less often (p = 0.006).
- The largest single gap is Haiku at the fee of 4: 75 percent with "withhold" last against 94 percent with it first.

Order is counterbalanced within every cell, so these effects do not bias comparisons between cells. They should be reported as a small primacy effect in a two-option choice, the opposite of the last-option artifact Huang finds in yes-or-no verdicts.
